The Industrial Revolution was a profound transformation in technology, economy, and society that began in Britain in the late eighteenth century and spread across Europe and North America during the nineteenth century. It marked the shift from hand production methods to machines, the rise of mechanized factories, and the growing use of steam power. Innovations such as James Watt's improvements to the steam engine, the spinning jenny, and the power loom greatly increased the speed and scale of manufacturing, especially in the textile industry.

Industrialization brought rapid urbanization as people moved from rural areas to cities in search of work. Towns grew around factories, and new transport networks—canals, railways, and steamships—linked raw material supplies to cities and opened markets. While economic output and standards of living eventually rose for many, the early decades of the Industrial Revolution were marked by difficult working conditions: long hours, low pay, child labor, and unsafe environments.

The social and political consequences were significant. A new industrial working class emerged alongside a growing middle class of factory owners, merchants, and professionals. These changes spurred debates about labor rights, public health, and education, and led to early social reforms. Over time, technological progress and legal reforms improved workplace conditions, while industrial capitalism reshaped global trade, deepening colonial resource extraction and fostering international economic networks.

The legacy of the Industrial Revolution is mixed: it laid the foundations for modern economic growth, mass production, and technological innovation, but also introduced environmental changes and social inequalities that societies continue to address. Its inventions and institutions set the stage for the modern world, influencing patterns of work, migration, and daily life that persist today.
